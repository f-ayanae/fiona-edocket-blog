    CycleSignal

    AI powered cycle tracking for earlier PCOS risk awareness. CycleSignal brings together a wearable, a short monthly check-in and two machine learning models to spot a concerning pattern early, explain why in plain words, and suggest when to see a GP. It is not a diagnosis.

    October 2026Team of fourPython · Streamlit
    The problem

    Polycystic ovary syndrome affects about 1 in 10 women of reproductive age, and the WHO estimates up to 70% are undiagnosed. Each symptom is easy to explain away, and a GP sees a snapshot rather than months of cycles.

    What we built

    A Streamlit prototype that combines a wearable cycle model, a risk model trained on self-reported answers, and a Claude-based assistant that explains the score and suggests when to see a GP.

    The result

    Test ROC-AUC of 0.92 and recall of 0.81. An ovulation detector failed its pre-set checks, so temperature is shown as context only. The app never diagnoses.

      Assessing a credit bureau score ahead of Germany's new scoring rules

      What a bank needs to check before it uses a credit bureau's new score, and how far public information gets you.

      Fiona Edocket13 Nov 202612 min read
      At a glance
      The problem
      From 20 November 2026, German law stops credit scores from using address data, age and several other kinds of information, and gives lenders new duties when they turn someone down. Most lenders use a score bought from a credit bureau. They did not build it and cannot see inside it, but they are still responsible for it.
      This project
      I took one real bureau score and asked what a bank would need to see before using it, and how much of that anyone can check from the outside.
      The answer
      Not yet, but there is a clear path to yes. Two pieces of evidence decide it, and the tests to run on them are already built.
      Key findings
      • One criterion, worth up to 94 points, uses how long you have lived at your address. The new ban on address data may cover it.
      • Someone with a clean record can score at most 743 after one year, and needs two to five years to reach the top class.
      • From 20 November, lenders must tell people more when they turn them down.
      What I built
      A Python pipeline: an evidence register, a points calculator, decision rules written in code, and a ready-to-run test kit tried on 303,949 real loans.

      Why I looked at this

      On 20 November 2026, a new rule in Germany's data protection law, §37a BDSG, starts to apply. It bans credit scores from using a person's age, gender, name, bank account transactions or address, among other things. At the same time, a change to §30 BDSG means a lender that turns down a loan because of credit bureau data has to tell the applicant more about why. In October, Germany's Federal Court of Justice also set out when a bureau has to explain an individual score.

      Most banks do not build their own credit scores. They buy them from a bureau. That left me with a simple question: what should a bank be able to show before it switches to a new bureau score, and how much of that can anyone check from the outside?

      The score

      In March 2026, a large German credit bureau replaced its old scores with one new score. It runs from 100 to 999 and is built from twelve published criteria. Every lender that uses it now has to decide whether to adopt it and where to set its cut-offs.

      To keep things concrete, I used a made-up lender, Mainkai Direktbank: a Frankfurt bank that would use the score to approve, price and size personal loans, about 120,000 applications a year. The bureau is real, but I do not name it.

      Why it matters

      Under German banking rules, a bank stays responsible for a model even when someone else built it. The supervisor's rulebook, MaRisk AT 4.3.4, says bought-in models need the same care as models built in-house: checks that they fit, that the data is sound, that they have been validated and that they can be explained. Another section, BTO 1.2 Tz. 5, says a bank must form its own view of credit risk rather than lean only on outside ratings.

      This bureau is unusually open. It publishes every criterion and the points each one is worth. That made it a good test of an easy assumption: if a model is transparent, it must be well evidenced. The bureau does not publish a model card, a validation report or any data on how its scores turned out, so I looked at what that gap means for a lender.

      How I did it

      I built the review in four parts.

      01
      Evidence register
      Every source I used (49) and every claim I made (30), each marked as either a sourced fact or my own judgement. The law and the BaFin circular are kept as original files, each with a digital fingerprint (a hash) so anyone can check they have not changed.
      02
      Points calculator
      The bureau's published points table, rebuilt in code and tested: scores really do run from 100 to 999, and points move the way they should. Where the bureau leaves gaps, the calculator shows the lowest and highest possible values instead of guessing.
      03
      Tests on real loans
      There is no public outcome data for this score, so I ran the tests a bank should ask for on 303,949 loans from Bondora, an Estonian lender. These results show how the tests work. They say nothing about the bureau's own score.
      04
      Decision rules
      Four possible answers (reject, defer, approve with conditions, approve), applied in code to the findings, so the recommendation comes from the evidence rather than a judgement call at the end.

      What I found

      The address question

      The new law bans address data ("Anschriftendaten") in scores, under §37a(2) No. 1(d). One of the bureau's criteria, how long you have lived at your current address, is worth up to 94 points. Read broadly, time at an address is address data, so it is banned. Read narrowly, the law is aimed at scoring people by where they live, and time at an address is simply a sign of stability. No court has decided yet.

      I do not treat this as a breach. I rated it High, and it becomes Critical if the bureau keeps the criterion and the bank's lawyers take the broad reading.

      New credit files hit a ceiling

      Criteria that depend on time make up 280 of the 999 points. Someone with a perfect payment record and no mortgage can score at most 743 after one year in the German credit system, which is Class 2. Reaching the top class takes between two and five years. The published table is not precise enough to narrow that down.

      It is normal for short credit histories to score lower. The real question is whether a given score means the same risk for a young person or a newcomer to Germany as it does for everyone else. Nothing public answers that.

      The bank's own loans move its customers' scores

      In the calculator, taking out a 60-month Mainkai loan lowers the borrower's score by 78 to 88 points, through three separate criteria. That matters when the bank offers top-up loans, and when it explains to customers that a longer loan can affect their credit standing.

      Averages can hide problems

      On the Bondora data, the ranking broke down at the risky end, just where a bank would set its automatic decline line. In 2025, grade F loans (17.6% default rate, 1,309 loans) defaulted less often than grade E loans (19.6%). Seven of the eight grades got worse compared with the reference period, yet the overall default rate barely moved (8.3% against 8.6%), because more loans landed in the best grade. Across customer groups, actual defaults ranged from 0.72 to 2.34 times what the grade predicted.

      What lenders must now tell people

      From 20 November, the amended §30(7) gives lenders two new duties. If a consumer loan is refused because of bureau data, the lender must tell the applicant what that data was, which database it came from and which kinds of data were used. If the decision was automated, the lender must also explain the right to have a person review it and how to challenge it. Failing to do so is an administrative offence under §43(1) No. 2. I rated this High.

      So, should the bank use the score?

      Not yet, but there is a clear path to yes. That is not a judgement against the score. My review uses a simple rule, written into the code: a key area can only be rated if there is a visible result, such as a reported number, a published table or a stated review conclusion. A description of a process does not count. Data, method, testing and the outside review all pass this rule. Testing on the bank's own applicants does not, so the rules return "defer pending evidence".

      The more useful question is what would change that. Running the same rules on two what-if scenarios shows that the answer turns on two pieces of evidence:

      Reject

      Both are realistic to get. The first is a standard request: the bureau scores 24 to 36 months of the bank's past applications with both the new and the old score. The second is a written answer from the bureau and a legal opinion from the bank's lawyers. Both are in the evidence request that comes with the review, as items E02 and E01.

      The tests are ready

      The bank would not need to design the tests when that data arrives. They are already built and run end to end on the Bondora data, so the same code can be pointed at the bank's own file:

      • Old score against new. A side-by-side comparison of how well each score separates good loans from bad ones (the Gini coefficient) on the same applicants. It passes if the new score is no more than 3 points worse. On the Bondora data, a strong score (0.37) against a weak one (0.18) gives a gap of +0.20, with a range of +0.19 to +0.21: a clear pass.
      • Later data. The same measure on a later period than the one the score was set on.
      • Ranking and calibration. Default rates by score band, with confidence ranges, flagging any band that does better than the safer band above it.
      • Customer groups. Actual against expected defaults for each group. On the Bondora data these are country, age band and similar; on the bank's file they would include young applicants and people with short credit histories.
      • Stability. How much the mix of applicants shifts from quarter to quarter, against a steady starting period.

      When the evidence arrives, the review picks up where it left off: run the tests, re-rate that one area, and let the same rules give the answer.

      How I built it

      The review is a Python project rather than a document. Every figure, rating and recommendation is produced by code from the evidence and the data, so changing a source or a threshold updates the report automatically. It runs in six stages:

      1. SourcesStatute, MaRisk circular, bureau pages, case law, commentary
      2. Evidence register49 sources and 30 claims, SHA-256 hashed
      3. Calculator labPoints table encoded and tested
      4. Outcomes lab303,949 Bondora loans, aggregates only
      5. Decision engineTyped findings, rules 1 to 4, scenarios
      6. OutputsReview, memo, crosswalk, calculator, CSVs

      Engineering

      • A typed data model. Findings, review areas and obligations are pydantic models with built-in checks, so an invalid review cannot be built. The rule about visible results, for example, lives in the model itself:
      # src/assurance/models.py
@model_validator(mode="after")
def _ratability_test(self):
    if self.core and self.ratable != bool(self.visible_result.strip()):
        raise ValueError(f"element {self.element}: core elements are ratable "
                         "if and only if a visible result is recorded")
    return self
      • Figures generated, not typed. The report text carries placeholders such as [[ceiling_12m]] that are filled from the lab results when it is built. The build stops if any placeholder is left empty.
      • Evidence you can check. Each source records a fingerprint of the excerpt I relied on and, where I hold the original document, of the file itself.
      • Testing. 53 pytest tests cover the calculator, the outcome tests and the decision rules.
      • Output. Jinja2 templates produce the HTML, and Playwright prints the PDFs. The calculator is a single self-contained web page.

      Statistics

      • Separating good from bad. Gini (2 × AUC − 1) with 200-draw bootstrap ranges, on a later period.
      • Old against new. A paired bootstrap of the Gini difference on the same applicants, against a 3-point tolerance.
      • Stability. The population stability index by quarter, with a check for a break in the reference period.
      • Calibration. Default rates with Wilson intervals by grade, and actual against expected by customer group.
      • Privacy. Any group with fewer than 30 loans is hidden, and no loan-level data leaves my machine.

      What I learned

      Fixed pass marks do not work for bought-in scores. The bureau reports a Gini of about 0.6 across everyone it scores. On one lender's applicants that number will always be lower, however good the model is. The fair test is a side-by-side comparison with the bank's current score, on the same people.

      Drift checks need a steady starting point. My first reference period mixed two different rating systems, and I only noticed when I looked at the data quarter by quarter.

      LimitsThis shows a method, using public evidence only. I had no access to the bureau's data, code or contracts. The Bondora results describe a different lender and a different population; I only show groups of at least 30 loans, and I do not share the raw data. The findings, ratings and tolerances are my own judgement.

      The same review in Ireland

      Germany's changes start on the same day the EU's revised Consumer Credit Directive (Directive (EU) 2023/2225) begins to apply across member states: 20 November 2026. An Irish lender adopting a bureau score would face many of the same questions under a different supervisor. The method carries over unchanged; the legal anchors do not.

      TopicGermany (this review)Ireland
      Data that may not be used§37a BDSG: age, gender, name, account transactions, address data and other categoriesCCD2 Art. 18: no special-category data and no social-network data, and the assessment may not rest on credit history alone. The address question is specific to §37a.
      Explanation and human review§30(7) BDSG notice duties when a loan is refusedCCD2 Art. 18: where the assessment is automated, a right to human review and a clear explanation
      Status of the rulesIn force from 20 November 2026CCD2 takes effect through national law. At the time of writing I could not confirm that Ireland's law has been published.
      Credit dataPrivate bureau score, 100 to 999The Central Credit Register reports credit data but produces no score; any score comes from a private provider or the lender itself
      Supervisor's rulesBaFin MaRisk AT 4.3.4, AT 9, BTO 1.2Central Bank of Ireland: revised Consumer Protection Code in force since 24 March 2026; outsourcing guidance and DORA for third-party and ICT arrangements
      AI ActCredit scoring is high-risk under Annex III 5(b) in both countries. Following the AI Omnibus, these obligations are reported to apply from 2 December 2027.

      In an Irish version of this review, the address finding would fall away, the notice-duty finding would rest on the directive's rights to an explanation and a human review, and the governance findings would follow Central Bank expectations instead of MaRisk. The rule about visible results, the old-against-new comparison and the customer-group tests would apply as they are.
